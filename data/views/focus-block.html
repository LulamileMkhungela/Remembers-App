The Quiet Work

The ninety-minute block

Attention does not run on a to-do list. It runs in cycles — and the cycle is roughly an hour and a half long.
Saved to Reading List · filed under: focus, habits

Most people plan their day in half hours and then wonder why the afternoon dissolved. The problem is not discipline. It is that the unit of planning does not match the unit of attention.

The workable unit is about ninety minutes: long enough to get somewhere, short enough that you do not need a heroic amount of willpower to start. Two of those in a day is a good day. Three is exceptional and you should not plan for it.

The four rules

Setup
  1. Decide the night before what the first block is for. One sentence, on paper.
  2. Do not open email, chat or the news before the first block ends. Not "quickly".
  3. Put the phone in another room. Face down is not another room.
  4. Keep a paper list beside you. Anything that arrives mid-block goes on the list, not into your day.

That fourth rule is the one people skip, and it is the one that makes the other three survivable. The list is where the interruption goes to wait. The point is not to suppress the thought — it is to stop the thought from becoming a tab.

Switching tasks costs fifteen to twenty minutes of recovery. Four switches in a morning is most of the morning.

What the numbers say

Self-reported deep work across a working week clusters around three hours a day for people who are deliberately protecting it, and close to one for people who are not. That gap is not talent. It is mostly calendar shape.

Which means the useful question is not "how do I focus harder" but "where in my week are the ninety-minute holes, and what am I putting in them by default".

Practical version: block 08:00–09:30 and 10:00–11:30 in your calendar as busy, with the meeting the block is for in the subject line. If someone tries to book over it, they can see what it is for.
